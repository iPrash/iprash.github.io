Agentic AI architect · Design phase field guide

Agentic underwriting for the London specialty market

This is the working guide for the design phase of an agentic underwriting programme. Lead with one idea: the decision ladder. Use code first, then deterministic rules, then an LLM only where a step needs reasoning, and a human where confidence is low. Prove that ladder on real broker submissions before the design phase ends. Everything else on this page supports that idea.

What "done" looks like at the end of the design phase
  • A blueprint the engineering team can build from. Target architecture, building blocks, agent cards, data model, integration points and non-functional requirements.
  • A chosen lighthouse. One use case for the MVP, with a baseline measured today and a target that the business signed.
  • Recorded decisions. One ADR per significant choice, so nobody re-argues them in engineering.
  • Evidence. A spike run on real, anonymised submissions that shows the ladder works and shows where it fails.

Self-check: eighteen questions

Answer each one aloud in under 30 seconds before you open it. Do it once now and again at the end of week 1. A miss sends you to the card named in the answer.

Triage

Where are the gaps?

1. What is the difference between the Lloyd's market and the company market?

Lloyd's is a marketplace of syndicates, each run by a managing agent, with business placed by accredited brokers. The company market is insurance companies in London that are not part of Lloyd's. Brokers often place one risk across both. Card A2.

2. Name the three ways a submission can arrive, by placement type.

Open market (one risk, negotiated on its own), facility (a pre-agreed arrangement such as a line slip that many similar risks attach to), and delegated authority (a coverholder or MGA writes risks under a binding authority and reports by bordereaux). Card A3.

3. What is a statement of values, and what does COPE stand for?

The SOV is the broker's schedule of every insured location with its values. COPE is construction, occupancy, protection and exposure: the four groups of risk characteristics an underwriter needs per location. Card A5.

4. Why does geocode resolution change the price?

Perils such as flood and coastal wind change sharply over short distances. A postcode centroid can sit in a different flood zone from the building. A coarse geocode forces the model to assume, which widens uncertainty and usually loads the price. An address that does not geocode returns no modelled loss at all. Card A6.

5. Name the four modules of a cat model.

Hazard (with its stochastic event set), vulnerability (damage functions), exposure (what is insured, where), and financial (applies policy terms to damage). Card A7.

6. What is AAL, and what does an EP curve show?

AAL is the average annual loss: the expected cat loss per year, the base of the technical price. An EP curve shows the probability that losses exceed each amount in a year. Read it as a return period: a 1-in-200 loss has a 0.5% annual chance. Card A7.

7. State the decision ladder in one sentence.

Structural cleaning in code, then deterministic rules, then an LLM with a confidence score only where reasoning is needed, then a human when the score is under the threshold, with an audit trail and a four-eyes check before anything goes to the cat model. Card D1.

8. A column header says "BLDG". Which rung of the ladder handles it?

First a synonym rule. If "BLDG" is in the dictionary, it maps to building TIV with confidence 1.0 and no LLM is called. If it is new, the LLM proposes a mapping with a score. A human confirms if the score is low, and a confirmed mapping becomes a new rule. Card D4.

9. When should a step not be an agent?

When it is deterministic: a calculation, a lookup, a fixed rule, a format conversion. Make it a tool or a service. An agent earns its place on summarising, extracting from unstructured documents, mapping ambiguous fields, retrieval over guidelines, and planning. Card E1.

10. Name five orchestration patterns and one use for each.

Sequential or prompt chaining (extract, then validate, then map). Routing (by class of business). Parallel (sanctions, appetite and duplicate checks at once). Orchestrator-workers (split a 40-tab SOV by tab). Evaluator-optimizer (a checker agent reviews the mapping). Add the human gate. Card E2.

11. Why is a first-in, first-out queue a problem here?

A one-building submission waits behind a 2,000-location schedule. Urgent, high-value work has no way to jump the line. The target uses triage and lanes. Card D3.

12. Why is sanctions screening a global domain service and not a regional agent?

The sanctions data is global, and the check is the same everywhere. One shared service avoids each region building and maintaining its own. Card B1.

13. What is the canonical exposure model you would propose, and why?

OED, the Open Exposure Data standard curated by Oasis LMF. It is open, model-agnostic, has a validation and conversion toolkit, and converters exist to and from vendor formats. Card D8.

14. Is commercial property underwriting AI "high-risk" under the EU AI Act?

No. Annex III names risk assessment and pricing for natural persons in life and health insurance. Commercial property is not on the list. Governance still matters for audit, the regulators and Lloyd's. Card H1.

15. What is the cheapest labelled dataset you already have?

The back-office team's past work. Every raw SOV that went in and every cleaned file that came out is an input and expected-output pair. That is your eval set and your rule source. Card E5.

16. Which comes first: the firm order or the signed line advice?

The firm order. The insurer is on cover from that point. The signed line advice follows, sometimes after a lag, and gives the final share after any signing down. Card A9.

17. Why does the UK and London market variant keep exposure management and cat modelling when the mid-market design does not show it?

It handles more high-value, complex commercial risks, so more submissions need modelled exposure. Its target is low to mid touch. No-touch is not the aim. Card B4.

18. What is a colleague decision point?

The programme's term for a human-in-the-loop step (colleague in the loop): a decision a person must make, surfaced in the underwriter workspace. Cards B4, B5.

Commercial insurance basics

An insured pays a premium to transfer a risk to an insurer. The insurer agrees to pay losses from named perils, up to a limit, above a deductible. Commercial property insurance covers buildings, contents and the income lost when a site cannot trade. Specialty lines cover larger or unusual risks: marine, energy, aviation, political risk, cyber.

A2 · London market

Lloyd's and the company market

The London market has two parts. Lloyd's is a marketplace of syndicates. The syndicates write the risks. Each syndicate is run by a managing agent, which employs the underwriters. Business comes in through Lloyd's brokers and through coverholders. The company market is the insurance companies that write business in London outside Lloyd's. A broker often places one large risk across both.

Words you will hear

  • MRC: the market reform contract, the standard slip format. It holds the risk details, terms and each insurer's signed line.
  • Lead and follow: the lead agrees terms first. Followers take smaller lines on the same terms.
  • The box: an underwriter's desk in the Lloyd's underwriting room.
  • Electronic placing: platforms such as PPL Next Gen and Whitespace carry the slip digitally.
  • CDR: the core data record, the market's agreed data set for a placement. It is built on ACORD standards.

Why it matters for the design

  • When your insurer leads, it needs the full cat analysis. When it follows, it may rely more on the lead's terms. Ask whether the pipeline treats lead and follow differently. [ask]
  • The same building can arrive twice, once through each route. Duplicate detection belongs in triage.
  • Market data standards (CDR, ACORD) give you field names for placement data. Do not invent your own.
Gotchas
  • Blueprint Two, the Lloyd's digital programme, was sunset on 19 March 2026. Do not describe it as the future plan. The CDR work carries on, and the market associations now push ACORD-based data standards and platform connectivity ver.
  • "Open market" has two meanings: a placement type (see A3) and, loosely, business that comes straight to a company outside Lloyd's. Ask which one people mean.
Say this"Lloyd's is a subscription market. A lead sets terms and followers take lines, much like a syndicated loan. So the data we produce must support both a leading decision and a following decision."

A5 · Data

The statement of values (SOV)

The SOV is the broker's list of every location to be insured, with its values and characteristics. It is the main input to the cat model. It comes in any shape: an Excel workbook, a CSV, a PDF table, sometimes a scanned page. The underwriter needs it in one canonical form before anything can be modelled.

What a location row holds

  • Identity and address: location number, street, city, postcode, country.
  • Values: building, contents, business interruption (BI), in one currency.
  • COPE: construction, occupancy, protection (sprinklers, fire brigade), exposure (neighbouring hazards).
  • Secondary modifiers: year built, number of storeys, roof type, floor area. Survey findings.

  • LocNumber, StreetAddress, PostalCode, CountryCode
  • Latitude, Longitude
  • OccupancyCode, ConstructionCode, YearBuilt, NumberOfStoreys

A6 · Data

Geocoding and resolution

Geocoding turns an address into a latitude and longitude that a model can use. Cat model vendors call it the gateway to all risk analysis. An address that does not geocode returns no modelled loss. When a geocoder cannot match at the highest resolution, it falls back to the next lower level, such as postcode or city. The model then assumes where the building is.

Resolution, best to worst

  1. Building or rooftop: the coordinates of the actual building.
  2. Address, interpolated: placed along a street between known numbers.
  3. Street: somewhere on the street.
  4. Postcode: the centroid of the postcode area.
  5. City or admin area, then country.

Public geocoders report this too. Google returns a location_type of ROOFTOP, RANGE_INTERPOLATED, GEOMETRIC_CENTER or APPROXIMATE. Treat the resolution as a confidence score for the location field.

UK-specific help

  • Every addressable place in Great Britain has a UPRN, a persistent property ID.
  • Ordnance Survey AddressBase links postal addresses to UPRNs with property-level coordinates. OS Open UPRN is a free dataset of UPRN positions.
  • A UPRN in the canonical record makes repeat submissions for the same building easy to match.

Why it changes the price

Flood and coastal wind hazard change over a few hundred metres. A postcode centroid can sit in a different flood zone from the building. A wide uncertainty band means a more cautious price.

Gotchas
  • Different tools use different geocoding engines, so the same address can move between underwriting and cat modelling. Use one geocoder of record and store its version with the result.
  • An LLM must never invent coordinates. It may propose a cleaner address to re-geocode, and it must cite the evidence for it.
  • Set the target resolution per peril and per value. A high-TIV coastal site needs rooftop. A low-value inland site may not.
Say this"I treat geocode resolution as a confidence score. The pipeline iterates: tool first, then a research agent proposes a better address, then a human confirms, and only then do we ask the broker, with one batched question."

A7 · Modelling

Cat modelling in one card

A catastrophe model simulates thousands of plausible events, such as storms, floods and earthquakes, against the insured locations. It estimates the losses. It looks beyond the short history of actual events. The output feeds the price, the accumulation view and the reinsurance buy.

Outputs to know

  • AAL: average annual loss. The expected cat loss per year. It is the cat part of the technical price.
  • EP curve: exceedance probability. The chance in one year that loss exceeds each amount. OEP uses the largest single event in a year. AEP uses the total of all events in a year.
  • Return period: 1 divided by the probability. A 1-in-200 loss has a 0.5% annual chance.
  • PML: probable maximum loss, often quoted at a return period such as 1-in-250.

Market and tools

  • Two large commercial vendors dominate: Moody's RMS (Intelligent Risk Platform) and Verisk.
  • Oasis LMF is an open-source, not-for-profit modelling framework. It curates the OED standard.
  • Vendor platforms offer APIs for geocoding, hazard lookups and model runs. That is how the pipeline calls them as tools.

Perils

Windstorm (with storm surge), flood (river, surface, coastal), earthquake, wildfire, severe convective storm (hail, tornado), and for some lines, terrorism and cyber.

Gotchas
  • The cat model is a deterministic engine owned by the exposure management team. Never let an agent "estimate" a modelled loss. Agents prepare the input and summarise the output.
  • Model versions change results. Store model version, settings and run ID with every quote.
Say this"The cat model stays the cat model. Our job is to get high-resolution, validated exposure into it in minutes instead of days, and to explain the result to the underwriter."

A8 · Pricing

Pricing and the quote

The cat model output goes into the pricing engine. The engine combines it with other loss elements and loads to give a technical price. The underwriter then decides the actual price and terms. The exact formula is the insurer's own. Learn the general shape and ask the pricing team for theirs.

Building block of a technical priceWhere it comes from
Cat loss cost (from AAL)Cat model
Attritional (non-cat) loss costPricing model, loss history
Expenses and acquisition costs (brokerage)Finance assumptions
Cost of capital and reinsuranceCapital model, reinsurance programme
Profit marginBusiness plan

The underwriter compares the technical price with the market price (what the broker expects), checks appetite and authority, and either quotes, declines, or refers. The rate adequacy (quoted price divided by technical price) is a common measure.

Gotchas
  • Pricing engines and their models are actuarial property. Integrate with them through their APIs. Do not rebuild pricing logic in prompts.
  • Straight-through processing on price is a business decision with authority rules. Design it as a rules gate with explicit limits.

A9 · London market

Quote, firm order and signed line

After the quote, the London market uses its own words for the steps to binding. Learn them, because the bind process is designed around them. The lead underwriter sets the terms, and other underwriters follow on the same or similar terms.

StepWhat it means
Indicative quoteAn early, non-binding view of price and terms. It tells the broker whether a firm quote is worth asking for.
Firm quoteA full quote is a firm offer that the insured can accept.
Written lineThe written line is what an underwriter offers.
Firm orderThe insured, or their broker, accepts the quoted terms. This signals that the insured wants to proceed and that the broker should confirm the placement.
Signing downWritten lines can add up to more than 100% when a risk is oversubscribed. Each underwriter's share is then reduced in proportion.
Signed lineThe final, adjusted share. It sets each underwriter's exact share of premium and claims.
SubjectivitiesConditions attached to a quote or a line, such as a survey to follow. They are tracked through placing and must be cleared.

What the workshop confirmed

  • The firm order comes first. At that point the underwriter knows the insurer is on cover.
  • The signed line advice comes later, sometimes after a delay.
  • When the signed line arrives, match it to the quote, flag any change in the line, and update the policy admin system.
  • A follower still does its own data extraction and risk assessment, even though the leader set the terms.

Why it differs by placement type

  • Open market: a classic broker and underwriter negotiation. More steps before a line goes down.
  • Line slip: the broker asks for a share, such as 5% or 10%, and the underwriter signs a line.
  • Facility: the insurer has pre-agreed to take a set share when risks meet set parameters.
  • Delegated authority: a different flow again (see A3).
Gotchas
  • Design the bind step to hold two numbers per insurer: the written line and the signed line. They differ when a slip is oversubscribed.
  • A follower's process can be lighter than a leader's, but it is never zero. Do not assume followers skip assessment.

Part B. The programme context

Three cards. What the wider programme is trying to do, how your track fits into it, and the shape of the phase you are in.

B1 · Strategy

Regional platforms, global domain services

The strategy moved from one global platform to regional platforms. Each region builds what its market needs, across underwriting, claims and policy management. A capability becomes global only when it is truly global, and then each region reuses it as a domain service.

The test for global versus regional

  • Global when the data is global and the rule is the same everywhere. Sanctions screening is the example: sanctions lists are global, and the check does not change by region.
  • Regional when the process, regulation, market practice or language differs. Lloyd's placement, facilities and bordereaux are London-market specific.
  • Shared agent, regional context when the task is the same but the rules differ. SOV cleaning is a shared agent. The broker conventions, currencies and appetite are regional context.

What this means for you

  • Before you design a capability, check whether a global service already exists or is planned. [ask: list of global services]
  • When you find something that looks global, propose it to the programme. Do not build it inside your region.
  • Record every reuse decision as an ADR.
Say this"Global only where the data and the rule are global, like sanctions. Everything else is a shared agent with regional context. That keeps us from repeating the single-platform mistake."
B2 · Architecture idea

Same agents, different context

Each line of business, region and product has its own process. Earlier attempts forced them into one system and it cost a lot. The new idea: keep one registry of reusable agents. Feed each one a market context at run time. The business keeps its variation. The architecture does not multiply.

Background and foreground

Map every step of today's process. Most steps run in the background: agents and code gather, clean, check, enrich, model and draft. A smaller set stays in the foreground, where a person reviews an agent's result and approves it, rejects it, or approves it with a comment. As an order of magnitude, the programme expects most steps to move to the background. [your baseline: steps today, steps in background]

Your first job on the UK track

  • Take the base design and the agent registry from the global work.
  • For each agent: reuse, reconfigure, retire for this market, or add a new one.
  • Write the UK and London market context pack.
  • Keep the background and foreground split explicit for every step.
Say this"We harmonise the agents. Context carries the business variation."

D1 · Core idea

The decision ladder

Every step in the pipeline starts on the lowest rung that can do it reliably. A step climbs a rung only when the rung below cannot decide. This keeps cost down, keeps results repeatable, and puts the LLM only where reasoning is needed.

Rules for climbing

  • Rung 1 runs on everything. It is plain code, such as Python with a spreadsheet library. It produces a clean table plus a log of what it removed.
  • Rung 2 applies everything the business already knows. A rule match scores 1.0 and needs no LLM.
  • Rung 3 runs only on what rung 2 could not decide. Its output is constrained to allowed values and carries evidence and a score.
  • Rung 4 gets only items under the threshold for that field's criticality.

Why this is the right shape

  • Cost. Token spend tracks LLM calls. Agent bills can exceed a budget in hours. The ladder caps calls by design.
  • Repeatability. Rules give the same answer every time. Auditors and underwriters trust that.
  • Learning. Human corrections move down the ladder into rules, so the LLM share falls over time.
  • Explainability. Every value records which rung set it and why.
Say this"Code for structure, rules for what we know, the LLM only where we need reasoning, and a human when confidence is low. Every decision is logged, and a four-eyes check happens before anything reaches the cat model. When a rules engine can decide, we go straight through."

Triage, priority and lanes

Replace the single first-in, first-out queue with a scored queue and three lanes. Small, clean work flows past large work. Urgent, high-value work goes first. Aging stops any case from waiting forever.

D4 · Target

Confidence scores and thresholds

A confidence score decides whether a value goes straight through or to a person. The score must mean something measurable: "at this score, the value is right this often on our test set". Set the threshold per field, by how much damage a wrong value can do.

Where the score comes from, by rung

RungScore source
1 Structure1.0 when parsing succeeds. A failure goes to the human queue with the reason.
2 Rules1.0 for an exact dictionary or code-list match. A fuzzy match scores by similarity, with a cut-off.
3 LLMA combined score. (a) Agreement across several samples or two different models. (b) Output constrained to the allowed code list, with "unknown" allowed. (c) Validation checks pass: valid code, value in range, fields consistent with each other. (d) Totals reconcile with the broker's stated TIV.
GeocodingThe geocoder's resolution level, compared with the target for that peril and value.

Thresholds by field criticality

TierFieldsThreshold policy
CriticalTIV values, currency, address and coordinates, perils coveredHigh threshold. Any change above a set share of TIV also needs four eyes.
ImportantConstruction, occupancy, number of storeys, year builtMedium threshold. Batch the reviews.
SecondaryRoof type and other secondary modifiersLower threshold. "Unknown" is acceptable; the model has defaults for it.

How to set the numbers

  1. Build a labelled set from past work: raw SOVs and the cleaned files the team produced (E5).
  2. Run the pipeline on it. For each field, plot accuracy against score.
  3. Choose the threshold where accuracy meets the target for that tier. Write the target down with the business.
  4. Start conservative. Lower the threshold only with evidence from production sampling.
Gotchas
  • Do not use the model's own "I am 90% sure" as the score. Self-reported confidence is poorly calibrated. Use agreement, validation and reconciliation.
  • Report two numbers together: the automation rate and the error rate at that threshold. A high automation rate with a hidden error rate is the worst outcome.
Say this"A confidence score is only useful if it is calibrated against our own past work. We set thresholds per field by the cost of being wrong, and we report automation rate and error rate side by side."
D5 · Target

Human review and the four-eyes check

The human step must be fast and must not turn into rubber-stamping. Show the person the evidence with the answer. Capture every correction as data.

A good review item shows

  • The source: file, sheet, cell range, and a snippet of the original.
  • The proposed value and the next-best alternatives.
  • Which rung proposed it, the score, and the reason.
  • The impact: share of TIV affected, and whether it changes the model result.
  • Actions: approve, correct, reject, each with an optional comment.

Controls

  • Four eyes (maker-checker) on critical changes before the cat model run. A second person approves.
  • Sampling: a small random share of auto-approved values goes to review anyway. This measures the hidden error rate.
  • Override tracking: if reviewers accept everything, the queue is too easy or nobody is looking. If they reject a lot, the threshold is too low.
  • Feedback loop: a correction confirmed twice for the same broker becomes a rung-2 rule, through a reviewed change.
Gotchas
  • Batch review items by type (all "occupancy" questions together). Context switching slows reviewers.
  • The reviewer's comment is the most valuable data you collect. Make it one click to add a reason code.

E1 · Core

Agent, workflow or tool?

Choose the simplest option that works reliably. Anthropic separates workflows, where code defines the path and LLMs fill steps, from agents, where the LLM directs its own steps and tool use. Microsoft's guidance gives the same spectrum: a direct model call, then a single agent with tools, then multi-agent orchestration. Use the lowest level that meets the need.

An LLM step earns its place for

  • Summarising: a broker email or survey into a risk summary.
  • Extracting from unstructured input: PDF tables, slips.
  • Mapping ambiguous fields to a code list.
  • Translating: foreign-language schedules.
  • Retrieval over guidelines and wordings (RAG).
  • Planning: splitting a complex SOV into work units.

Keep it a tool when it is

  • A calculation: TIV totals, currency conversion.
  • A lookup: sanctions list, geocoder, code list.
  • A fixed rule: authority limits, appetite rules.
  • A model run: cat model, pricing engine.
  • A format conversion: CSV to Parquet, OED version upgrade.
Say this"An agent that only discovers an API and calls it is an expensive API client. If a rules engine can decide, we go straight through and pay nothing for tokens."

E5 · Quality

Evals: how you prove it works

An eval gives the system a task and grades the result. Anthropic separates capability evals (can it do the task at all) from regression evals (did a change break something). Graders can be code, a model, or a human. Use all three.

Your best assetThe back-office team has years of finished work. Each raw SOV that came in and each cleaned file that went out is an input with its expected output. Ask for a sample of these pairs in week 1. It is your eval set, your source of rules, and your baseline.

Metrics per run

  • Field accuracy by tier, against the cleaned file.
  • TIV reconciliation: pipeline total versus expected total.
  • Resolution mix of geocodes.
  • Automation rate: share of fields set on rungs 1 to 3 without a human.
  • Human review rate and review time.
  • Elapsed time per lane. Cost per submission.

How to grade

  • Code graders for exact fields: values, codes, totals.
  • Model graders for summaries and broker drafts, with a rubric. Allow the grader to answer "unknown".
  • Human graders for a sample, to calibrate the model graders.
  • Run the suite on every prompt, model or rule change, before release.
Gotchas
  • Past cleaned files contain human errors too. Have the SME correct a small "gold" subset.
  • Anonymise before data leaves the secure environment. Check the data-handling rules first.

E6 · Operations

Observability and cost control

  • Trace every run: each step, tool call, model call, tokens, time and result, linked to the submission ID.
  • Budget per submission, with alerts and a hard stop. A loop that runs unwatched can spend a month's budget in hours.
  • Route models by task: small, cheap models for classification; larger models only for hard reasoning.
  • Cache repeated context, such as the context pack and broker templates.
  • Batch work that is not urgent, such as bordereaux, at lower cost.
  • Watch the LLM call rate per submission. It should fall as corrections become rules. If it does not fall, the feedback loop is broken.
  • Compare with the human cost. If agents cost more than the people they support for a task, the task belongs on a lower rung.
Say this"Token cost is an architecture concern. I design for it with the ladder, model routing, caching and budgets, and I report cost per submission next to time and accuracy."

F7 · Build

Your own spike: SOV to OED in an afternoon

Set up

Steps

  1. Rung 1. Read every sheet with formatting. Drop hidden rows and columns, blank rows, and subtotal rows. Record fill colours. Find the header row.
  2. Rung 2. Map headers with a synonym dictionary. Normalise currency and numbers. Check ranges.
  3. Rung 3. Send only unmapped headers to an LLM. Ask for a choice from the allowed OED fields or "unknown", with a short reason. Run it twice and score agreement.
  4. Rung 4. Write low-score items to a review CSV. Correct them by hand. Add the corrections to the dictionary.
  5. Validate. Write an OED location file and run the OED checker.
  6. Measure. Automation rate, accuracy against the expected output, time, tokens.

Rung 1 skeleton

from openpyxl import load_workbook

def read_sheet(path, sheet):
    wb = load_workbook(path, data_only=True)   # cached values, not formulas
    ws = wb[sheet]
    hidden_cols = set()
    for d in ws.column_dimensions.values():   # one entry can cover a range
        if d.hidden and d.min:
            hidden_cols.update(range(d.min, d.max + 1))
    rows = []
    for r in ws.iter_rows():
        n = r[0].row
        if ws.row_dimensions[n].hidden:
            continue
        cells = [c for c in r if c.column not in hidden_cols]
        values = [c.value for c in cells]
        if all(v in (None, "") for v in values):
            continue
        fill = cells[0].fill.fgColor.rgb if cells[0].fill and cells[0].fill.fill_type else None
        rows.append({"row": n, "values": values, "fill": fill})
    return rows, [str(m) for m in ws.merged_cells.ranges]

Why insurance changes agent design

J5 · Rule

Agent or tool? Classify every step

Calling every step an agent inflates cost, test effort and governance load. Classify each step with four questions. General version in E1.

  1. Is the answer fixed by a table, formula or lookup? Then it is code or a rule. Make it a tool.
  2. Does it call one engine or service and pass the result on? Then it is a tool call inside a master agent.
  3. Does it need to read unstructured content, weigh evidence or plan several steps? Then it is an agent.
  4. Would two experts disagree on the answer? Then it needs a confidence score and a human decision point.

J6 · Deep

Orchestrating long-running underwriting work

Use two levels of orchestration. They solve different problems, so do not force one tool to do both.

Process level: the workflow engine

  • A durable state machine per submission: received, cleared, triaged, exposure ready, priced, quote approved, quoted, firm order, line signed, bound, issued.
  • Handles timers (chase a broker after two working days), human tasks, retries with back-off and compensation (undo steps) when a later step fails.
  • Writes the audit trail. Survives restarts. Waits for weeks at no cost.

Agent level: master agents

  • Short reasoning loops inside one stage.
  • Call workers as typed tools: a task in, a result out. No free-text chat between agents.
  • Use the patterns in E2: sequential chains, routing, parallel fan-out and fan-in, evaluator loops with an iteration limit.

Three shapes that recur in underwriting

Sequential with gatesIntake, enrich, validateEach step must pass before the next starts; gaps go to a clarification queue

Quote approvalEvery quote versionPremium, rating factors, rationale, version comparisonApprove, amend, decline

Instructed taskWork that templates cannot doA bounded instruction from the underwriterComplete, query
Technical escalationA tool or engine fails after retriesThe error and retry historyRetry, route to support (underwriters never see it)

Design rules

  • Every item carries its evidence. Nobody should need to open another system to decide.
  • Route by the authority matrix. A colleague cannot approve above their authority.
  • Record every action and correction as labelled data for evaluation.
  • Track override rate per agent and per field. A high rate means the agent needs work.
  • Give each queue an owner and a service level.

J9 · Data

Data, standards and memory

Business objects

  • Submission: facts with source and confidence, document references, clearance, triage.
  • Exposure: locations, values, COPE, geocodes, hazard, model results, defaults applied.
  • Quote: structure, versions, every engine run, rating factors, rationale, approval.
  • Policy: bound contract, signed line, contract data record, documents.
  • Reference: clauses, appetite, authority, guidelines, precedents.

Standards to align with

  • ACORD data standards for messages and records.
  • The London market CDR (Core Data Record) for the contract data at bind. Watch for local documents that reuse "CDR" for something else.
  • OED (Open Exposure Data) for exposure, so one object can feed several cat models. See D8.

J10 · Integration

Legacy systems of record: wrap, shadow, replace

Pattern 1: wrap and strangle

  • Move high-touch work (intake, triage, enrichment, documents) into agents and services first.
  • Leave complex, low-touch calculations and the legal record in the legacy system.
  • Replace one capability at a time, after a parallel run proves the new path.

Pattern 2: business tools over adapters

  • Group tools by business domain (submission, party, exposure, pricing, documents, policy), for example as MCP servers.
  • Each tool declares read or write. Writes take an idempotency key, so a retry cannot book a policy twice.
  • Read paths first; write paths after the decision points are proven.

J12 · FinOps

Cost per submission

Agent cost grows with the number of steps and calls in each job. One submission can fan out into dozens of model and tool calls. Measure cost against the business unit the firm cares about.

  • Unit: cost per submission and per bound policy, broken down by agent and by model.
  • Biggest saver: the decision ladder. A deterministic step makes no model call.
  • Model routing: small models for classification and checks; larger models for rationale, options and hard documents.
  • Token tactics: cache stable prompts and tool definitions; load tools on demand; summarise history; compress tool outputs; cap output length per task.
  • Budgets: a token and step budget per agent run, with alerts before the limit.
  • Sequence: first route all model traffic through one gateway and capture usage; then attribute cost and publish dashboards; then add budgets and chargeback.
Say this"We will report cost per submission from the first release, by agent and by model. If we cannot see it, we cannot control it."
